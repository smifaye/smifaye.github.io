We moved our volunteering and complaints forms to a new system and redesigned them. Across both, completion rates rose by over 30% over 6 months, compared with the previous 6 months.

## The problem

Both forms were on an old system that we were retiring. We needed to move them to a new system, give them new designs and make sure they met current accessibility standards.

## My role

I worked in a multidisciplinary team with software engineers, user researchers, product designers and subject matter experts. I worked with the product designers on the question fields, help text and flow across both forms. I worked with the user researchers to turn their findings into the content, including how we ask for someone’s name.

## What we did

We followed the design system’s principles for forms. Each page asks one thing. Labels sit above fields, and we only add hint text when it’s needed. Error messages are specific rather than generic, and each form ends with a “check your answers” page.

![Screenshot of the form asking for the user's address details](/images/address-details.png)

Hint text is where the content does the most work. On the phone number question, the label says “Phone number (optional)”. The hint says: “If not in the UK, include the country code. For example, +34 for Spain.” It doesn’t assume everyone has a UK number, and it gives an example so no one has to guess the format.
### Getting names right

Names turned out to be the most important part of the project. Our research found that:

- People found separate first and last name fields easy to use and mostly preferred them, probably because that’s what they’re used to seeing.
- People were frustrated when they couldn’t use special characters, or weren’t sure a form would accept them.
- Many felt that online and offline forms are set up for western names.
- People were frustrated when a service adapted, misspelled or reordered their name, and many had experienced this.

From this we made two hypotheses. A single full name field would be more inclusive across nationalities and cultures. And name fields should accept the characters and symbols that people use to express who they are. Both supported Mission Two: closing the gap in access and experience for marginalised people.

We couldn’t move to a single full name field, because the legacy systems that receive the data need a first name and a last name. So we changed what we could. ### Confirmation emails

I wrote the confirmation emails for both forms: one to confirm we’ve received a complaint, and one to confirm an application to volunteer. I kept them simple and told people what happens next.

## The result

Across both forms, completion rates rose by over 30% over 6 months, compared with the previous 6 months. We changed the system, the design and the content at once, so I can’t say how much of the rise came from each.

## What happened next

The designs and structure are now part of the design system. Any new form we create follows them.

## What I learned

Inclusive design often means working within constraints. Where we couldn’t change the structure of a form, we changed what it accepted and how it spoke to people.
